Twenty-eight mornings before a recovery flag means anything

A recovery ring or strap prints one heart-rate-variability number every morning and colors it against a baseline built from your own readings: the last seven mornings held against a four-week reference of twenty-eight. The first useful flag needs twenty-eight mornings of data before it exists. Price the rig at $300 with a four-year service life and that is 1,460 nights of hardware at 21 cents a morning, $5.75 of which is spent before the app can say anything worth hearing.

The window arithmetic is where the noise gets in. Price a normal morning at 65 milliseconds and a red-eye at 40. Inside the seven-night weekly average, that one bad night drags the mean by 25 over 7, about 3.6 milliseconds, which is 5.5 percent of normal. The same night inside the twenty-eight-night reference moves it 25 over 28, about 0.9 milliseconds, 1.4 percent. The weekly view is exactly four times more sensitive to a single bad night, because its divisor is four times smaller.

Set the back-off flag at ten percent under baseline, which at 65 milliseconds sits at 58.5. A genuine recovery morning landing at 58 clears the line by half a millisecond. The red-eye's 3.6-millisecond pull on a weekly-smoothed number is seven times that margin, so the flag can flip on a flight schedule rather than on physiology. The monthly reference costs four weeks of patience and buys 0.9 milliseconds of drag per bad night instead of 3.6.

Skipping mornings has a meter running too. Two missed readings a week leave the seven-night window running on five samples, moving each surviving morning's weight from 14 percent to 20 percent, and over a year it drops 104 nights, a 28 percent dent in the record. A suppressed week, the seven-day illness kind, sits in the reference at one twenty-eighth per night for a month and drags the trigger about a millisecond in the sick direction, so the app asks for less while the body is still paying.
